# Manifest Destiny: the game plan

A side-scrolling platformer that follows America's story from the Pilgrims at Plimoth to a country joined
coast to coast. Each world is a region and an era, and the trip moves west the way the country did.

- **Who it's for:** the history is written for 2nd to 5th graders (about ages 7 to 11). The platforming, the
  secret exits, the railroad challenge levels and the hidden journal pages are there for older kids and grown-ups.
- **What it teaches:** where things are (regions, states, the map), when things happened (a year on every level),
  and why (a short story card as each level starts, and info boxes full of facts). Plus new words: colony, compact, frontier, territory,
  treaty, purchase, homestead, transcontinental.
- **The theme:** *Manifest Destiny* was a phrase first used in 1845. It meant the belief that Americans were meant to
  spread across the whole continent. The game follows that spread, but it is honest about who already lived on the
  land and what the spread cost them (see "Telling it honestly" below).

The old Manifest Destiny cast (Leo the leprechaun, Shamrock the unicorn, the goblins, King Grumbles and his gang)
is set aside. It lives on in **Lucky Leo**. Some of its moves and ideas come back in new, historical clothes: the
unicorn becomes a horse you unlock, the snail shells become box-turtle shells, the spiky hedgehogs become
porcupines, and so on.

---

## The story

**Real young people from history.** Each world brings in a new hero: a real child or teenager of that time and
place wherever history records one, each with a skill that suits their time. The first is **Wrestling Brewster**, a
real Mayflower boy who was about six when he sailed in 1620 with his father, Elder William Brewster. Each hero's card
says which parts are true and which are the game's. Real Native people appear as respected characters you meet
(like Tisquantum), not as characters you control.

**Each level opens with a story card**: a year, a place and one or two plain sentences ("September 1620. 102
passengers set sail from Plymouth, England, for America."). **The history is in info boxes**: blocks with an italic
*i*, 3 to 5 in every level. Bump one and its fact pops up (the game waits), and the level's **info meter** fills in.
**At the end of each region, after its boss, comes a quiz**: five questions picked at random from all of that
region's info boxes. Get all five right and the next region opens; a wrong answer shows the right one and offers a
fresh quiz.

**The villain: Silas Grabwell** (made up). He's a greedy land speculator who turns up in every era trying to grab
whatever's valuable first: furs, land, gold, the railroad. His **Grab Gang** run the boss fights alongside the
regional bosses below. He gives the story a cartoon bad guy who isn't a real group of people, and he stands for
the part of westward expansion that was about grabbing. The finale has him trying to steal the Golden Spike.

## Heroes: a new one in every region

You start as Wrestling Brewster. **Beat a world's boss (and its quiz) and the next region's hero joins you.** Once a hero has
joined, you can play any level as any hero. Some secrets can only be reached with a particular hero's skill, so
older players have a reason to go back.

| 2 Mid-Atlantic | **Sybil Ludington, Patriot rider** (real: at 16 she rode 40 miles in the night, 1777) | 1777 | Fastest runner and the best rider: on the horse she gallops and leaps further. Lantern lights dark levels. |

**Animal friends and rides** (replacing Shamrock the unicorn):

- **Liberty the horse** is unlocked at the end of World 1 (the Midnight Ride, 1775). Ride him for higher, longer
  jumps. A hit knocks you off instead of hurting you (as Shamrock did).
- Later rides: a birchbark canoe (World 5), a covered wagon (World 6), a pack mule in the mines (World 7), a

## Enemies: the creatures and hazards you'd really meet

No people are enemies, except cartoon villains (Grabwell's gang, pirates, a few redcoats in the Revolution who just
lose their hats). Native people are never enemies.

**Turkeys are in Worlds 1 to 4.** Wild turkeys were all over the East (Benjamin Franklin thought the turkey was "a
much more respectable bird" than the bald eagle). They strut along, gobble, and puff up and rush at you when you get
close.

| World | Creatures and hazards |
|---|---|
| 1 New England | **turkeys** ✅, wolves ✅, crows in the cornfields ✅, porcupines (spiky: ground pound only) ✅, box turtles (kick the shell) ✅, ship rats ✅, seagulls, crabs, lobsters; storm waves ✅, snowdrifts; redcoats ✅ (on patrol in Boston, and the redcoat major at the end) |
| 2 Mid-Atlantic | **turkeys**, raccoons, groundhogs that pop up, blue crabs and horseshoe crabs, hornets; redcoat drummers (bonk their hats off) |
| 3 Atlantic South | **turkeys**, alligators, snakes, wild hogs, mosquitoes, pelicans, Blackbeard's pirate crew; hurricanes |
| 4 Frontier South | **turkeys**, black bears, skunks, bobcats, possums, catfish; river snags and floods |
| 5 Great Lakes | **beavers** (their fur drove the whole fur trade), moose, wolverines, mosquito swarms, sturgeon; lake-effect blizzards |
| 6 Great Plains | **bison** (they charge in herds: jump them), prairie dogs that pop up, rattlesnakes, grasshopper swarms (the locust plague of 1874), jackrabbits; prairie fire, tornadoes |
| 7 Rocky Mountains | grizzly bears, mountain goats and bighorn sheep (headbutts), golden eagles, marmots; avalanches, geysers, rockslides |
| 8 Southwest | rattlesnakes, scorpions, Gila monsters, roadrunners, javelinas, coyotes, vultures, longhorn stampedes; tumbleweeds, flash floods |
| 9 Pacific | California grizzlies, sea lions, leaping salmon, banana slugs; claim jumpers (Grabwell's gang); runaway mine carts |

**Bosses** are a regional legend or wild beast, with a Grab Gang twist:

1. New England: **the redcoats** at Lexington and Concord, 1775, where World 1 ends and the Revolution begins. A cartoon
   British officer and his marching drummers: stomp him three times while the lanterns in the Old North Church say
   where he'll come from ("one if by land, two if by sea"), and his drummers just lose their hats
2. Mid-Atlantic: the **Jersey Devil** (folklore), with Grabwell at Fort McHenry (1814)
3. Atlantic South: **Blackbeard** (a real pirate, 1718)
4. Frontier South: a giant **river catfish** on the Mississippi
5. Great Lakes: **Grabwell's fur fort** and his "beaver baron" foreman
6. Great Plains: a **twister** that picks up the whole level
7. Rockies: a **grizzly** in the mountain pass
8. Southwest: a giant **rattlesnake**, and a longhorn stampede
9. Pacific: Grabwell's claim jumpers at Sutter's Mill, then **Grabwell himself at the Golden Spike** (the finale)

## The worlds, in history order

Each world is a region **and** an era, so the map is also a timeline. The trip moves the way the frontier did:
New England and the Atlantic coast first, then over the mountains, the Great Lakes, the Plains, the Rockies, the
Southwest and the Pacific. Every world's card says whose homelands it crosses.

### World 1: New England, the colonies (1620 to 1775)
*Wampanoag, Massachusett, Narragansett, Pequot, Mohegan, Nipmuc and Wabanaki homelands*

1. **The Mayflower** (1620): sail across the Atlantic and into Provincetown Harbor ✅ built
2. **Plimoth Plantation** (1621): the colony, the Wampanoag homesite, the first harvest ✅ built
3. **Cranberry Bog** (1622): the Wampanoag homeland, sassamanash, the seasons ✅ built
4. **A City upon a Hill** (1630): the Puritans build Boston; the cows on the Common ✅ built
5. **Providence** (1636): Roger Williams, the Narragansett, freedom of religion ✅ built
6. **The Connecticut River** (1639): Thomas Hooker, the Dutch post, the Fundamental Orders ✅ built
7. **Maple Sugaring** (1650): the Abenaki homeland in the Green Mountains, the Sugar Moon, birch-bark buckets ✅ built
8. **The Gulf of Maine** (1700): Pemaquid, cod, fish flakes and shallops ✅ built
9. **The Boston Tea Party** (1773): the streets of Boston, redcoats, the three tea ships and their tea chests ✅ built
10. **The Midnight Ride and Lexington and Concord** (1775) 🏰 boss: the redcoat major (one if by land, two if by
    sea), then the World 1 quiz ✅ built · still to come: **Liberty the horse**

*Secret-only levels (whaling and islands):* Martha's Vineyard (Noepe, from a golden flag over the Cranberry Bog) ✅,
Nantucket (the whaling capital, from the Mayflower's main top) ✅. Their facts are a bonus: the quiz asks only about
the main road's levels.
*Railroad depot:* the Granite Railway, Quincy (1826, America's first commercial railroad).

### World 2: The Mid-Atlantic, a new nation (1775 to 1830)
*Lenape, Haudenosaunee (Iroquois), Susquehannock, Nanticoke and Piscataway homelands*
Washington crossing the Delaware (1776), Independence Hall (the Declaration in 1776, the Constitution in 1787),
Valley Forge (1777), Slater Mill (1793, the start of factories), the Erie Canal (1825), Fort McHenry (1814, the
Star-Spangled Banner), Washington, D.C. Hero: **Patience, Patriot messenger**.
*Secret theme:* the Culper Spy Ring (invisible-ink clues on Long Island, 1778).
*Railroad depot:* Baltimore and Ohio, Baltimore (1828).

### World 3: The Atlantic South (1607 to 1830s)
*Powhatan, Cherokee, Catawba, Muscogee (Creek), Timucua and Seminole homelands*
Jamestown (1607: thirteen years before Plimoth, a flashback level), Pocahontas (Matoaka), Williamsburg, Yorktown
(1781, the end of the Revolution), Blackbeard's Outer Banks (1718), Charleston and Savannah, St. Augustine (1565,
the oldest European-founded city in the U.S., Spanish). Hero: **Ben the sailor**.
*Secret theme:* pirate coves and treasure maps.
*Railroad depot:* Charleston (1830, the "Best Friend of Charleston," the first regularly scheduled steam train).

### World 4: Over the mountains, the frontier South (1775 to 1840)
*Cherokee, Shawnee, Chickasaw, Choctaw and Muscogee homelands*
The Cumberland Gap and the Wilderness Road (1775), Kentucky and Tennessee, Mammoth Cave, the Mississippi
steamboats, the Louisiana Purchase at New Orleans (1803), Sequoyah's Cherokee writing system (1821), and the
Trail of Tears (1838). Hero: **Ruth, frontier scout**.
*Secret theme:* hidden trails and cave passages.
*Railroad depot:* Chattanooga.

### World 5: The Great Lakes, the Northwest Territory (1787 to 1850)
*Anishinaabe (Ojibwe, Odawa, Potawatomi), Ho-Chunk, Menominee, Miami, Sauk and Meskwaki homelands*
The fur trade, Fort Mackinac, Tecumseh and the Shawnee (1811), the Erie Canal's boats arriving on Lake Erie,
Chicago (1833), Lincoln's Illinois. Hero: **Jacques, voyageur and trapper**. Beavers everywhere.
*Secret theme:* voyageur canoe routes and portages. One secret-only level crosses into **Canada** (Grand Portage
to Fort William on Lake Superior, the fur trade's big summer meeting place).
*Railroad depot:* Chicago, the great railroad hub.

### World 6: The Great Plains (1804 to 1870s)
*Lakota, Dakota, Pawnee, Osage, Kaw, Omaha, Mandan, Hidatsa and Cheyenne homelands*
Lewis and Clark leave St. Louis (1804) and winter with the Mandan, Sacagawea joins them, the Oregon Trail's
jumping-off towns (1840s), the Pony Express (1860), the Homestead Act and sod houses (1862), the bison. Hero:
**Addie, pioneer**.
*Secret theme:* Pony Express relay stations.
*Railroad depot:* Omaha (1865, where the Union Pacific started west).

### World 7: The Rocky Mountains (1805 to 1869)
*Shoshone, Crow, Blackfeet, Ute, Nez Perce and Arapaho homelands*
Lewis and Clark cross the mountains with Sacagawea's help (1805), the mountain men's rendezvous, Independence
Rock and South Pass on the Oregon Trail, the Mormon Trail to Utah (1847), Pikes Peak (1858), building the railroad
through the mountains. Hero: **Lee, railroad builder**.
*Secret theme:* old mine shafts and hidden valleys.
*Railroad depot:* Promontory Summit (see the finale).

### World 8: The Southwest (1540 to 1880s)
*Pueblo peoples, Diné (Navajo), Apache, Comanche, Tohono O'odham and Hopi homelands*
Mesa Verde and the Ancestral Puebloans, the Spanish missions, Santa Fe (1610), the Santa Fe Trail (1821), Texas
when it was part of **Mexico** and the Alamo (1836), the war with Mexico (1846 to 1848, when this whole region
changed countries), the cattle drives (1867), the Grand Canyon (1869). Hero: **Rosa, vaquera**.
*Secret theme:* the old Spanish trail, El Camino Real. One secret-only level is in **Mexico** (where the trail
began, in Chihuahua).
*Railroad depot:* Santa Fe.

### World 9: The Pacific (1840s to 1869)
*Chinook, Coast Salish, Miwok, Maidu, Ohlone and Nisenan homelands*
The end of the Oregon Trail (1843+), Sutter's Mill and the Gold Rush (1848 to 1849), San Francisco, the Central
Pacific climbing the Sierra Nevada (built mostly by Chinese workers), and the **Golden Spike** at Promontory Summit,
Utah, on May 10, 1869, where the two railroads met and the country was joined coast to coast. Hero: **Sam,
forty-niner**.
*Secret theme:* gold mines. One secret-only level is the **Klondike** in Canada's Yukon (1896, the last great gold rush).
*Railroad depot:* Sacramento (1863, where the Central Pacific started east).

**After the finale (bonus worlds):** Alaska (bought from Russia in 1867) and Hawaii (1898), and an epilogue card
on how all 50 states came to be (Alaska and Hawaii became states in 1959).

## Secrets and the railroad

**Secret exits fit their region**, and **each one leads to a level you can only reach from that secret exit.** It's
never just a shortcut to an ordinary level. Each world has one or two, shown as a grey star on the map until found.

| World | How the secret exit fits | Secret-only level |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | a whaling flag at the top of the rigging; a ferry | Martha's Vineyard, Nantucket |
| 2 | a spy's invisible-ink note | The Culper Ring (Long Island) |
| 3 | a pirate's treasure map | Blackbeard's Hidden Cove |
| 4 | a hidden trail blaze on a tree | The Long Hunter's Cave |
| 5 | a canoe portage | Grand Portage → Fort William (Canada) |
| 6 | a Pony Express relay horse | The Lost Relay Station |
| 7 | an old mine shaft | The Hidden Valley |
| 8 | a Spanish trail marker | El Camino Real (Chihuahua, Mexico) |
| 9 | a gold seam in the rock | The Klondike (Yukon, Canada) |

**The railroad (our "Star Road").** America's railroads are the fast way around the map, the way they really tied
the country together.

- **Each world has a railroad depot**, a little station on the map, opened by finding that world's **golden
  spike**. The spike is hidden in one of the world's levels (a harder secret than a secret exit).
- **Ride between any two open depots** in seconds: a steam train chuffs across the map along the tracks.
- **Each depot has a Railroad Challenge level**: short, hard and set in the railroad age (1826 to 1869). These are
  the toughest levels in the game, for older players. Beat all nine and you get a gold engine on the title screen
  and the "Iron Horse" ending.
- The track on the map is drawn in as you open depots, so by the end there's a line across the whole country,
  like the real transcontinental railroad.

## The map

- **Canada and Mexico are on the map as land** (shown muted and labelled), not water, so the U.S. sits where it
  really is. You only play there where it makes sense along the way: the secret-only levels in Canada (Fort
  William, the Klondike) and Mexico (Chihuahua).
- Each world is a region in its own colour. Worlds you haven't reached are grey, and a year badge sits on every
  level, so the map also works as a timeline.
- Levels still being built are wooden signposts you walk past.

## Telling it honestly

This is a game about Manifest Destiny for children, so it has to be truthful as well as fun.

- **Native nations are people with their own names, homes and history, never enemies.** Every world names whose
  homelands it crosses. Native characters appear as themselves: guides, teachers, traders and leaders, based on
  well-documented real people where possible (Tisquantum and Ousamequin/Massasoit of the Wampanoag, Pocahontas,
  Sequoyah, Tecumseh, Sacagawea). They're drawn plainly and respectfully, in the clothing of their own time and
  nation, with no headdress clichés.
- **The facts say what happened,** at a 2nd to 5th grade level: the Wampanoag helped the Pilgrims survive; disease
  had already killed most of the Patuxet; land was taken by treaties that weren't kept; the Cherokee were forced
  west on the Trail of Tears; the bison were nearly wiped out.
- **Enslaved people**: the Atlantic South and the frontier South worlds say plainly that enslaved Africans and
  African Americans did much of the work there, against their will. The game does not make a game level out of
  escaping slavery.
- **"Manifest Destiny" itself** gets its own card on the title screen and the ending: who said it (John O'Sullivan,
  1845), what people believed, and that the people already living in the West saw it very differently.
- Before the later worlds are built, check the facts and pictures against educator resources such as the
  Smithsonian's *Native Knowledge 360°* and Plimoth Patuxet Museums' Wampanoag Homesite.

## Learning features (to add as we go)

- **Story cards** at the start of each level (year, place, one or two sentences). ✅
- **Info boxes**: 3 to 5 per level, each a fact with a quiz question; an info meter on screen and on the map. ✅
- **The region quiz** after each region's boss: five questions from its info boxes, all five right to go on. ✅
- **History Journal**: every info box you've found, level by level, to read again any time.
- **Timeline** strip on the map screen, with a marker for where (and when) you are.
- **Read-aloud** button on story cards and facts, for younger players.
