# Manifest Destiny: the game plan

A side-scrolling platformer that follows America's story from the Pilgrims at Plimoth to a country joined
coast to coast. Each world is a region and an era, and the trip moves west the way the country did.

- **Who it's for:** the history is written for 2nd to 5th graders (about ages 7 to 11). The platforming, the
  secret exits, the railroad challenge levels and the hidden journal pages are there for older kids and grown-ups.
- **What it teaches:** where things are (regions, states, the map), when things happened (a year on every level),
  and why (a short story card as each level starts, and info boxes full of facts). Plus new words: colony, compact, frontier, territory,
  treaty, purchase, homestead, transcontinental.
- **The theme:** *Manifest Destiny* was a phrase first used in 1845. It meant the belief that Americans were meant to
  spread across the whole continent. The game follows that spread, but it is honest about who already lived on the
  land and what the spread cost them (see "Telling it honestly" below).

The old Manifest Destiny cast (Leo the leprechaun, Shamrock the unicorn, the goblins, King Grumbles and his gang)
is set aside. It lives on in **Lucky Leo**. Some of its moves and ideas come back in new, historical clothes: the
unicorn becomes a horse you unlock, the snail shells become box-turtle shells, the spiky hedgehogs become
porcupines, and so on.

## The story

**Real young people from history.** Each world brings in a new hero: a real child or teenager of that time and
place wherever history records one, each with a skill that suits their time. The first is **Wrestling Brewster**, a
real Mayflower boy who was about six when he sailed in 1620 with his father, Elder William Brewster. Each hero's card
says which parts are true and which are the game's. Real Native people appear as respected characters you meet
(like Tisquantum), not as characters you control.

**Each level opens with a story card**: a year, a place and one or two plain sentences ("September 1620. 102
passengers set sail from Plymouth, England, for America."). **The history is in info boxes**: blocks with an italic
*i*, 3 to 5 in every level. Bump one and its fact pops up (the game waits), and the level's **info meter** fills in.
**At the end of each region, after its boss, comes a quiz**: five questions picked at random from all of that
region's info boxes. Get all five right and the next region opens; a wrong answer shows the right one and offers a
fresh quiz.

**The villain: Silas Grabwell** (made up). He's a greedy land speculator who turns up in every era trying to grab
whatever's valuable first: furs, land, gold, the railroad. His **Grab Gang** run the boss fights alongside the
regional bosses below. He gives the story a cartoon bad guy who isn't a real group of people, and he stands for
the part of westward expansion that was about grabbing. The finale has him trying to steal the Golden Spike.

## Enemies: the creatures and hazards you'd really meet

No people are enemies, except cartoon villains (Grabwell's gang, pirates, a few redcoats in the Revolution who just
lose their hats). Native people are never enemies.

**Turkeys are in Worlds 1 to 4.** Wild turkeys were all over the East (Benjamin Franklin thought the turkey was "a
much more respectable bird" than the bald eagle). They strut along, gobble, and puff up and rush at you when you get
close.

## The level timer

Like Super Mario World's: the clock counts down a little faster than seconds (300 on most levels, 400 on long ones, 500
on the longest, 250 on Railroad Challenges). At 100 it's HURRY UP!, at 0 it's TIME UP (a life lost), and the time left
at the goal (or a boss) is a bonus of 50 points a count. It stops while a fact or the quiz is up, and the menu can turn
it off for the youngest players.

## Secrets and the railroad

**Secret exits fit their region**, and **each one leads to a level you can only reach from that secret exit.** It's
never just a shortcut to an ordinary level. Each world has one or two, shown as a grey star on the map until found.

| World | How the secret exit fits | Secret-only level |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | a whaling flag at the top of the rigging; a ferry | Martha's Vineyard, Nantucket |
| 2 | a spy's invisible-ink note | The Culper Ring (Long Island) |
| 3 | a pirate's treasure map | Blackbeard's Hidden Cove |
| 4 | a hidden trail blaze on a tree | The Long Hunter's Cave |
| 5 | a canoe portage | Grand Portage → Fort William (Canada) |
| 6 | a Pony Express relay horse | The Lost Relay Station |
| 7 | an old mine shaft | The Hidden Valley |
| 8 | a Spanish trail marker | El Camino Real (Chihuahua, Mexico) |
| 9 | a gold seam in the rock | The Klondike (Yukon, Canada) |

**The railroad (our "Star Road").** America's railroads are the fast way around the map, the way they really tied
the country together.

- **Each world has a railroad depot**, a little station on the map, opened by finding that world's **golden
  spike**. The spike is hidden in one of the world's levels (a harder secret than a secret exit).
- **Ride between any two open depots** in seconds: a steam train chuffs across the map along the tracks.
- **Each depot has a Railroad Challenge level**: short, hard and set in the railroad age (1826 to 1869). These are
  the toughest levels in the game, for older players. Beat all nine and you get a gold engine on the title screen
  and the "Iron Horse" ending.
- The track on the map is drawn in as you open depots, so by the end there's a line across the whole country,
  like the real transcontinental railroad.
- ✅ Built: all nine depots and the track are on the map (faint and dashed where it isn't open yet, with
  "coming soon" stations); a station screen to play the depot's Railroad Challenge or ride the train; the Quincy
  and Baltimore depots and their challenges. The main line runs Quincy, Baltimore, Chicago, Omaha, Cheyenne,
  Sacramento, with branches south to Charleston and Chattanooga and down to Santa Fe.

## The map

- **Canada and Mexico are on the map as land** (shown muted and labelled), not water, so the U.S. sits where it
  really is. You only play there where it makes sense along the way: the secret-only levels in Canada (Fort
  William, the Klondike) and Mexico (Chihuahua).
- Each world is a region in its own colour. Worlds you haven't reached are grey, and a year badge sits on every
  level, so the map also works as a timeline.
- Levels still being built are wooden signposts you walk past.

## Telling it honestly

This is a game about Manifest Destiny for children, so it has to be truthful as well as fun.

- **Native nations are people with their own names, homes and history, never enemies.** Every world names whose
  homelands it crosses. Native characters appear as themselves: guides, teachers, traders and leaders, based on
  well-documented real people where possible (Tisquantum and Ousamequin/Massasoit of the Wampanoag, Pocahontas,
  Sequoyah, Tecumseh, Sacagawea). They're drawn plainly and respectfully, in the clothing of their own time and
  nation, with no headdress clichés.
- **The facts say what happened,** at a 2nd to 5th grade level: the Wampanoag helped the Pilgrims survive; disease
  had already killed most of the Patuxet; land was taken by treaties that weren't kept; the Cherokee were forced
  west on the Trail of Tears; the bison were nearly wiped out.
- **Enslaved people**: the Atlantic South and the frontier South worlds say plainly that enslaved Africans and
  African Americans did much of the work there, against their will. The game does not make a game level out of
  escaping slavery.
- **"Manifest Destiny" itself** gets its own card on the title screen and the ending: who said it (John O'Sullivan,
  1845), what people believed, and that the people already living in the West saw it very differently.
- Before the later worlds are built, check the facts and pictures against educator resources such as the
  Smithsonian's *Native Knowledge 360°* and Plimoth Patuxet Museums' Wampanoag Homesite.
